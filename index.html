Content Archetypes: Clustering a Search Portfolio into Actionable Groups

What natural performance archetypes exist across a large content inventory — and can they be discovered directly from search behavior, without hand-written rules?

LaneStructured Content Archetype Clustering MethodK-Means, standardized features Data windowMarch 2026 (mid-panel) StatusFinal
Abstract

Five sentences, no theory

This project asks whether a large content inventory can be grouped into meaningful performance archetypes using only observed search signals, rather than hand-written rules. Using a March 2026 slice of FlyRank's search warehouse (331,437 content items across 55 clients), four leakage-checked features — impressions, clicks, average position, and click-through rate — were standardized and clustered with K-Means. The resulting six clusters separate cleanly along visibility and position, surfacing groups such as champions, buried pages, and hidden gems that a single-threshold rule could not distinguish. A held-out, client-grouped validation check confirms that this structure holds beyond the exact rows it was fit on. The result is offered as a decision-support triage lens for content review — not a causal claim about what makes any single page succeed.

01 — Introduction

The decision this supports

A content team managing tens of thousands of pages cannot review each one individually. The practical question is not "what predicts a page's decline?" but simpler and more immediate: which group of pages should a reviewer look at first, and what should they do with each group?

A fixed rule — like a single CTR or freshness threshold — can only check one or two conditions at a time. Real archetypes are defined by the interaction of several signals at once: a page can have high traffic and low engagement, or low traffic and surprisingly strong engagement, and a single-threshold rule collapses these into the same bucket. Clustering was chosen specifically because it can separate pages by the combination of signals, not just one axis — while remaining fully interpretable through cluster-level averages.

What this is not: this is not a claim about why any individual page ranks the way it does, and it does not reproduce or reveal anything about Google's ranking algorithm. It is a triage lens built from observed, pseudonymized search behavior.
02 — Data

What went in, and what was deliberately left out

All analysis runs on the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse release (build flyrank_pseudonymized_warehouse_release_v20260703), read directly from its hosted Parquet files — no raw client names, domains, URLs, or queries are present anywhere in this pipeline or its outputs.

The grain is one row = one content item, for one client, on one day, confirmed by query (total rows exactly equal distinct client × content × date combinations — no duplication). March 2026 was used as the development window on purpose: the warehouse's final month is held out as a sealed test period and was never used to shape any feature or label logic here.

Because only 4.2% of rows carry GA4 (engagement/session) data, engagement-based features were excluded from the final model — using them would have meant treating "no data" as "no engagement" for 95.8% of rows, which is not a safe assumption. The four features used are all Search Console signals, available for effectively every row in the slice: total impressions, total clicks, average position, and click-through rate.

FlyRank's own product decision outputs — health_score, priority_score, action_type, and any refresh/review flags — are excluded entirely. They are not observable signals; they are the product's own conclusions, and including them would let clustering simply reproduce an existing rule instead of discovering structure from raw evidence.

03 — Methodology

Baseline, model, and how each was checked

Baseline (Week 4). Before any modeling, two signals were checked directly against the data rather than assumed. CTR-vs-position confirmed cleanly: click-through rate drops as position tier worsens, across a large n in every bucket — matching the logic behind FlyRank's own CTR-fix flag. A simple rule was then encoded: flag pages with enough volume to matter, a fixable position (not top-3, not past position 20), and a CTR below their own position tier's average. This produced a ranked queue of 52,839 candidate pages with one reason code and one action label.

Model (Week 5). K-Means was chosen because the underlying question — "what natural groups exist?" — has no label to predict; it is an unsupervised question by construction. Features were standardized before clustering so that large-scale features like impressions could not silently dominate the distance calculation. k = 6 was selected by comparing silhouette scores across candidate values on a training split.

Validation (Week 6). The single biggest methodological risk for this data is that content items from the same client can share structural similarities — so a plain random split risks letting the model "see" a client's pattern in both train and test. The model was re-run under a client-grouped split (whole clients held out, never divided across train/test) and compared before vs. after:

Split designTrain silhouetteTest silhouette
Random split (Week 5, original)0.4870.480
Client-grouped split (honest)0.4750.490

The grouped split's test silhouette (0.490) sits close to — and slightly above — the random split's test silhouette (0.480). This is a reassuring, honest result: the random split was not overly optimistic, and the cluster structure holds up even when whole clients are held out of training. The archetypes appear to reflect real content-level behavior rather than a client-specific artifact.

A leakage audit was repeated on the final feature set: no feature is calculated from outside the March 2026 window, no FlyRank product flag was reintroduced, and no derived field secretly encodes information from a later period.

05 — Limitations & honest framing

What this can and cannot say

  • Observational, not causal. These clusters describe association between search signals, observed in one month. They do not show that moving a page between archetypes would cause a change in its performance.
  • One month is a snapshot. March 2026 alone cannot separate a real, lasting pattern from ordinary month-to-month noise or seasonality.
  • Unbalanced client history. Clients have very different amounts of tracked history; only a small subset have 12+ months of data, limiting any seasonal reading.
  • Outlier sensitivity. As shown above, K-Means is sensitive to extreme values; one page produced its own single-item cluster.
  • Metric clustering, not semantic clustering. Archetypes are grouped by numeric behavior, not by article meaning or topic — no text fields were available or used, and none of this should be read as content-meaning analysis.
  • Lost daily detail. Aggregating to one row per content item per month collapses day-to-day volatility — two pages with identical monthly totals but very different daily patterns look identical to this model.
06 — Ranked recommendations

An action playbook, by archetype

1st

Champions (SPC—04) — protect. High traffic, strong position, solid CTR. Avoid disruptive changes; monitor for early signs of decline only.

2nd

Hidden Gems (SPC—03) — promote / expand. Best CTR of any real cluster despite modest traffic; a proven format worth wider distribution or internal linking.

3rd

Buried Pages (SPC—02) — visibility work, not a metadata fix. Average position past 40 means the constraint is ranking, not the title or snippet.

4th

Mid-Tier Visible (SPC—00) — monitor on a normal cadence; steady performers, no urgent action implied by this data alone.

5th

Background / Low-Signal (SPC—01) — lowest priority for manual review; the largest group by count, best handled with lightweight, rule-based monitoring rather than individual attention.
